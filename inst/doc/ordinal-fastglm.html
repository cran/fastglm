Ordered outcomes and cumulative-link models with fastglm

Jared Huling

Outcomes such as satisfaction or symptom severity have a natural ordering, but the differences between their categories need not have a numerical interpretation. Ordinal regression estimates the probabilities of these categories while preserving their order. In fastglm, this includes common-slope and partial proportional-odds models, mean bias reduction, coefficient and model-assumption tests, probability summaries, and fitting from repeatable blocks of data.

Ordinal functionality at a glance

Models and estimation

All three cumulative links, logit, probit, and complementary log-log, support maximum likelihood (ML) and mean bias reduction through firth=TRUE. With the logit link, common slopes give proportional odds; allowing selected slopes to differ across thresholds gives partial proportional odds. The corresponding probit and complementary log-log specifications are nonparallel cumulative-link models.

Models and estimation

Common slopes and the cumulative-logit model

For categories numbered from lowest to highest, the default model fitted by fastglm_ordinal() is

\[ \operatorname{logit}\{\Pr(Y_i\leq j\mid x_i)\} =\alpha_j-x_i^\mathsf{T}\beta-o_i, \qquad j=1,\ldots,K-1. \]

The thresholds \(\alpha_j\) divide the outcome scale, and \(o_i\) is an optional offset whose coefficient is fixed at one. A common slope vector \(\beta\) applies to all thresholds. Consequently, a one-unit increase in a predictor multiplies the odds of an outcome above any threshold by the same factor, \(\exp(\beta_r)\). This common odds ratio gives the model its name. It does not imply equal changes in category probabilities.

Positive slopes shift probability toward higher categories under this sign convention, which also agrees with MASS::polr(). The thresholds already specify the model’s location, so a separate location intercept would be redundant. Formula fits remove the ordinary model-matrix intercept. A matrix supplied directly must omit that intercept.

Alternative cumulative links

The cumulative-logit model uses the logistic distribution to translate a threshold and predictor value into a cumulative probability. More generally, the model is \[ \Pr(Y_i\leq j\mid x_i)=G(\alpha_j-x_i^\mathsf{T}\beta-o_i). \] The probit link uses the standard normal distribution for \(G\). The complementary log-log link uses \(G(\eta)=1-\exp\{-\exp(\eta)\}\), whose asymmetric shape allows cumulative probabilities to approach zero and one at different rates. All three links retain ordered thresholds and common slopes. Positive slopes shift probability toward higher outcomes under the same sign convention.

fit_probit <- update(fit_br,link="probit")
fit_cloglog <- update(fit_br,link="cloglog")
link_models <- list(logit=fit_br,probit=fit_probit,cloglog=fit_cloglog)
link_averages <- do.call(rbind,lapply(names(link_models),function(link) {
    data.frame(link=link,fastglm_average(link_models[[link]],weights=housing$Freq))
}))

The fits are compared through their category probabilities because their coefficient scales differ. Exponentiated coefficients have the common-odds-ratio interpretation for the logit link. Reversing the outcome order changes a complementary-log-log model, so the declared ordering should reflect the substantive outcome scale.

Mean bias reduction uses the first and second derivatives of the selected cumulative distribution in the adjusted equations of Kosmidis (2014). It targets bias in that link’s threshold and slope parameters. The same link is retained by prepared fits, refitting bootstrap calculations, predictions, marginal effects, and model assessment. fastglm_po_test() tests the common-slope restriction on the fitted link scale; for probit and complementary log-log models its printed heading identifies that interpretation. These score and Wald tests retain their first-order inferential scope.

With two categories, the cumulative-link model is a binary regression for membership in the lower category. Under the convention above, its binary linear predictor is the threshold minus the location predictor and offset. The corresponding binomial GLM and ordinal model therefore solve the same mean bias-reduction equations after accounting for these signs. For binary probit and complementary log-log fits, bounded Fisher-scoring updates permit temporary increases in the score norm; requiring that norm to decrease at every step can prevent progress toward a solution. Convergence still requires a small adjusted score and a small remaining predictor change.

Partial proportional odds and nonparallel effects

A predictor can have a different association with moving above the lowest outcome category than with reaching the highest category. A partial proportional-odds model represents this difference by estimating separate slopes at the cumulative thresholds for selected predictors. The other predictors retain a common slope, which limits the number of parameters when only some associations require this flexibility.

For common predictors \(x_i\) and predictors \(z_i\) with threshold-specific effects, the model is

\[ g\{\Pr(Y_i\leq j\mid x_i,z_i)\} =\alpha_j-x_i^\mathsf{T}\beta-z_i^\mathsf{T}\gamma_j-o_i, \qquad j=1,\ldots,K-1. \]

With the logit link, \(\exp(\gamma_{jr})\) is the odds ratio for an outcome above threshold \(j\) associated with a one-unit increase in predictor \(z_r\), holding the other predictors fixed. The probit and complementary log-log links support the same specification, with effects interpreted on their respective link scales. We refer to these more generally as nonparallel cumulative-link models because their slope vectors can differ across thresholds.

The nonparallel argument selects the effects that vary. For the housing example, we allow contact with other residents to have separate effects on the odds of satisfaction above Low and above Medium. This illustrates the specification; the choice of terms should also reflect the scientific question and the information available to estimate the additional effects.

The range of valid predictions

Separate slopes can produce crossing cumulative probabilities at some predictor values. Valid category probabilities require each consecutive cumulative predictor to increase:

\[ \alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_j -z^\mathsf{T}(\gamma_{j+1}-\gamma_j)>0. \]

The common predictors and offset cancel from this difference. The fitter enforces this condition at positive-frequency training rows, and prediction checks it again at the requested rows. For continuous predictors with varying effects, extrapolation can violate this condition even when fitting converges. An invalid prediction produces an error identifying the affected rows; clipping probabilities or reordering thresholds would change the specified model.

The reported thresholds correspond to predictors and offset equal to zero. That combination can lie outside the valid prediction range, so the thresholds alone need not be ordered. fastglm_diagnostics() reports the smallest fitted cumulative-predictor gap, which assesses ordering at the observed predictor values. With type="link", predictions are a matrix containing one cumulative predictor per threshold. Applying the fitted inverse link to this matrix gives the cumulative probabilities.

Mean bias reduction with varying effects

Sparse category patterns can produce infinite maximum-likelihood estimates even when each category is represented somewhere in the data. Mean bias reduction adjusts the joint equations for thresholds, common slopes, and varying slopes. Because the adjustment uses their full expected information, it accounts for the parameters estimated together in the cumulative model.

The wine example in Kosmidis (2014) relates five ordered bitterness ratings to temperature during grape crushing and contact between juice and grape skins. We use the four groups of category counts from that example, with a common contact effect and threshold-specific temperature effects.

The original observations include repeated ratings by judges; the model-based covariance treats the represented observations as independent and does not account for dependence within judges.

Mean bias reduction is available for all three links and targets the original threshold and slope coefficients. It does not guarantee a finite solution or accurate small-sample Wald inference for every design. The diagnostics check the adjusted score, remaining predictor change, information conditioning, and probability ordering. A small score alone is insufficient when the remaining update or information indicates an unstable fit.

Nonparallel models require positive total frequency in every declared category. They support dense, prepared, and streamed fitting; the inference available afterward depends on whether observations are retained, as summarized in the support guide. The empty-category handling below applies to common-slope models.

Average probabilities describe a covariate distribution rather than a single profile. Continuous predictors and local changes

A continuous predictor also permits a local comparison: the rate at which each category probability changes as the predictor increases. This derivative is often called a marginal effect. Its size can vary across observations even when the model has a common slope, because the probabilities depend on the other predictor values. Averaging these derivatives summarizes the local association over a specified predictor distribution.

The housing predictors above are categorical, so their associations are described by discrete contrasts. For a continuous example, we generate an ordered severity outcome from an exposure and a binary group. The quadratic term allows the association with exposure to vary over its range, and the interaction allows that association to differ between groups.

set.seed(110303)
severity <- data.frame(exposure=runif(500,0,3),
                       group=factor(rep(c("A","B"),250)))
location <- with(severity,.7*exposure-.15*exposure^2+
                  .35*exposure*(group=="B"))
severity$outcome <- ordered(cut(location+rlogis(nrow(severity)),
    c(-Inf,.3,1.6,Inf),labels=c("low","moderate","high")))
severity_fit <- fastglm_ordinal(
    outcome~exposure+I(exposure^2)+exposure:group,
    data=severity,firth=TRUE)
exposure_effect <- fastglm_marginal_effects(
    severity_fit,"exposure",newdata=severity)

#> 3 exposure     high  0.07321807 0.01749777  0.03892307  0.10751308

The estimates are changes in probability per unit of exposure, averaged over these observations. Multiplying an estimate by a small exposure change approximates the corresponding average probability difference. A full one-unit change can differ appreciably from this local approximation. The effects sum to zero across categories because increases in some category probabilities are accompanied by decreases in others. A positive common slope shifts probability toward higher outcomes, but an intermediate category can gain or lose probability depending on the predictor values.

For formula fits, variable="exposure" refers to the original exposure variable. The calculation differentiates its linear term, squared term, and interaction together, using the fitted prediction basis. Matrix fits differentiate the named design column while holding the other columns fixed. Formula derivatives use small centered changes in the predictor; terms must be smooth within each row, and both perturbed values must lie in the transformation’s domain. Expressions that recompute sample summaries, such as I(exposure-mean(exposure)), are unsupported. Supplying newdata is necessary when the original predictor cannot be recovered from the stored model frame. Offsets are held fixed, including offsets expressed as functions of the selected predictor.

The intervals account for uncertainty in the thresholds and slopes through the delta method. They condition on the supplied predictor distribution and averaging weights; frequency weights must be supplied again when those frequencies define the desired average. The vcov. argument accepts an alternative coefficient covariance, including an eligible robust covariance from fastglm_vcov(). These intervals use a first-order normal approximation and may be unreliable in small or extreme samples.

The same calculation applies when exposure has threshold-specific effects. Each cumulative probability then has its own exposure slope, and the uncertainty calculation includes their joint covariance.

#> 2 exposure moderate  0.06068574 0.02194015  0.01768385  0.10368764
#> 3 exposure     high  0.05937875 0.02004914  0.02008316  0.09867434

These are local derivatives at the supplied predictor values. The fitted cumulative probabilities must be ordered at those values; a derivative does not establish that a larger exposure change remains within the valid prediction range.

Hypothesis tests and uncertainty

Coefficient tests ask whether specified effects or contrasts equal their hypothesized values. Tests of the common-slope restriction ask whether those effects can be shared across thresholds. We first consider coefficient inference and uncertainty calculations; the common-slope tests appear with model assessment.

Coefficient profiles and score tests

A Wald interval summarizes uncertainty using a coefficient estimate and its standard error. A profile-likelihood interval instead considers candidate values of that coefficient and re-estimates all other parameters at each value. Those other parameters are called nuisance parameters for this calculation: they remain part of the model, but they are not the focus of the interval. Profiling allows the likelihood to describe different amounts of uncertainty on either side of the estimate.

For maximum-likelihood ordinal fits with common or threshold-specific slopes, we can obtain profile intervals and test several coefficient values jointly. The following test assesses the contribution of housing type while re-estimating the thresholds and the other slopes.

For this adjusted-score procedure, the bias adjustment is defined by the full model, including the tested coefficients. The constrained fit solves its equations in the directions left free by the hypothesis. The test then uses the full-model information to account for nuisance estimation. The implementation and its first-order justification are described in ?fastglm_score_test.

Both score procedures assume independent observations or integer frequency counts, a fixed number of parameters, an interior true parameter, and nonsingular limiting information. The adjustment does not change the leading chi-square reference under these conditions. This establishes an asymptotic procedure without resampling; it does not establish a higher-order correction or exact small-sample calibration. Finite bias-reduced estimates alone do not establish these conditions. Ordinal mean bias reduction generally has no scalar penalized-likelihood objective, so profile-likelihood intervals remain restricted to ML fits.

These procedures also test linear hypotheses within a partial model. This allows the equality of selected threshold-specific slopes to be assessed while retaining other varying effects, as illustrated next.

Robust covariance and bootstrap uncertainty

The expected-information standard errors above use the fitted categorical distribution to describe sampling variation. Each observation also contributes a score, which measures how its log likelihood changes as the parameters change. A sandwich covariance combines the observed sensitivity of the score equations with the variation in these individual contributions, as described by Zeileis (2006). This permits a first-order variance estimate under distributional misspecification, provided observations are independent and the fitted parameter has a regular, identifiable population target. Robust standard errors do not correct an incorrect mean or probability model. Robust covariance is the safer choice when the link is in doubt: in simulations with a misspecified link, model-based threshold intervals covered between 92% and 97.5% of the time at a nominal 95%, while robust intervals stayed close to 95%.

The frequencies represent individual residents. A row with frequency ten contributes ten individual observations to this calculation. For mean-bias-reduced fits, the sandwich uses ordinary likelihood scores and their observed derivative at the bias-reduced estimate. With a fixed number of parameters and a regular, identifiable limit, the score adjustment becomes small relative to sampling variation as the sample size increases. This first-order convention differs from differentiating the complete adjusted equations and does not supply a finite-sample coverage guarantee. The returned covariance records this convention.

When observations belong to independent clusters, cluster supplies the cluster identifier for each fitted row. The calculation then uses the sum of score contributions within each cluster, following the construction described by Zeileis, Köll, and Graham (2020). All observations represented by a frequency row must belong to its stated cluster. A large number of independent clusters is needed for the usual approximation; repeated observations within a few clusters do not substitute for additional clusters. Cluster covariance is available through fastglm_vcov(fit, type="HC0", cluster=cluster_id). The current robust interface requires positive observed frequency in every declared category.

Each replicate records convergence, the empty categories, and whether its empty-category pattern differs from the original. An endpoint is the lowest or highest category of the ordered scale. Mean bias reduction keeps its threshold finite when an endpoint is empty, so the same set of model parameters remains available. For a parametric bootstrap with mean bias reduction, the package returns uncertainty summaries when the original fit and every refit contain all interior categories and all refits and statistics succeed. Percentile intervals also have simulation error, so a substantive analysis needs enough refits to estimate the interval endpoints adequately.

Prediction scores, calibration, and graphical slope comparisons

Good numerical convergence establishes that the estimating equations were solved, but the fitted probabilities also need to describe the outcome distribution adequately. Prediction scores summarize discrepancies, while calibration plots indicate where those discrepancies occur. Assessing predictions on observations excluded from fitting helps distinguish predictive performance from agreement with the training sample.

The housing data contain frequency rows rather than individual records. We randomly divide the residents represented by each row between training and evaluation samples, retaining the same predictor coding. The evaluation frequencies are used only after fitting the training model.

set.seed(61105)

evaluation_frequency <- housing$Freq-training_frequency
training_fit <- fastglm_ordinal(
    Sat~Infl+Type+Cont,data=housing,weights=training_frequency,
    firth=TRUE,tol=1e-10)
assessment <- fastglm_ordinal_assess(
    training_fit,newdata=housing,y=housing$Sat,
    weights=evaluation_frequency,bins=5)
assessment$scores
#>   frequency log_loss       rps rps_normalized
#> 1       545 1.038522 0.4246117      0.2123059

The logarithmic loss penalizes assigning small probabilities to observed categories. The ranked probability score sums the squared discrepancies between predicted cumulative probabilities and the individual indicators of an outcome at or below each threshold. Both are proper prediction scores: their expected values are minimized by the true outcome probabilities; see Gneiting and Raftery (2007). Smaller values indicate better predictions for the same outcome scale and evaluation observations. The normalized ranked probability score divides by the number of thresholds, placing its possible values between zero and one. For grouped counts, the calculation averages individual scores; squaring the difference from a grouped observed proportion would measure a different quantity.

plot(assessment,type="calibration")

For each threshold, the calibration plot compares the mean predicted probability with the observed proportion at or below that threshold. The bins are equal-width intervals of predicted probability, chosen without reference to the observed outcomes. Points near the diagonal indicate agreement within those bins. Sparse bins can vary considerably, and the frequency column in assessment$calibration records the number of observations represented. Empty bins have unavailable observed proportions. These plots are descriptive and can conceal discrepancies within a bin; they do not establish that the full conditional probability model is correct.

The proportional-odds model also assumes that a predictor has the same slope at each threshold. A descriptive comparison fits a separate binary model for an outcome above each threshold, retaining the original predictors and offsets. As illustrated in Harrell’s ordinal regression chapter, differences between estimates at different cutoffs can arise from sampling variation even when proportional odds holds. The auxiliary models use mean bias reduction to permit finite estimates in many sparse or separated samples.

Testing the common-slope restriction

We can assess the common-slope restriction with fastglm_po_test(). The score measures how the log likelihood would initially change if the slopes were allowed to differ across thresholds. Its expected variation is calculated from the category probabilities under the fitted proportional-odds model, with adjustment for the estimated thresholds and common slopes. This calculation uses expected Fisher information, obtained by averaging over possible outcomes analytically. It requires neither simulated outcomes nor resampling to compute a p-value.

po_test <- fastglm_po_test(fit_br)
po_test

#> Terms: Infl, Type, Cont
#> Null fit: maximum likelihood
#> Sample: 1681 observations represented by 72 rows
#> 
#>  Statistic df p-value
#>      8.729  6  0.1894
#> 
#> Note: First-order chi-square approximation.

By default, the test refits the proportional-odds null by maximum likelihood, including when the original model used mean bias reduction. The original fitted model and its predictions remain unchanged. The printed table identifies the null estimation method and uses a first-order chi-square approximation. Expected and observed information can give different score statistics in finite samples. The SAS/STAT description of the proportional-odds score test, on pages 4246 to 4248, allows either form; the implementation here uses expected information throughout.

The same test can be included with prediction scores by calling fastglm_ordinal_assess(fit_br,po.test="score"). Printing that assessment displays its scores and test in separate tables. Printing its proportional.odds.test component displays the test alone. For a report or subsequent calculation, as.data.frame(po_test) and broom::tidy(po_test) provide numeric columns with the full computed precision; display rounding does not change those values.

For \(s\) predictor columns and \(K\) categories, the joint test has \(s(K-2)\) degrees of freedom. A small p-value gives evidence against the common-slope model in the specified direction of departure. A large p-value does not establish proportional odds, particularly when the sample contains little information about a category or predictor. The graphical comparison remains useful for understanding which threshold-specific associations differ and whether those differences are substantively important.

The chi-square approximation requires independent observations and a correctly specified, identifiable null model with positive category probabilities. It can be inaccurate in small or sparse samples. Separation and a bias-reduced null

When a predictor group contains only the highest outcome, increasing its slope can keep improving the likelihood without reaching a finite maximum. This is a form of separation. Additional iterations cannot produce a finite ML estimate in such a sample. The test checks for this configuration in predictors with two observed values and reports it directly.

exposure <- matrix(c(0,1),ncol=1,dimnames=list(NULL,"exposed"))
sparse_counts <- rbind(c(12,18,20),c(0,0,4))
colnames(sparse_counts) <- c("low","middle","high")
sparse_fit <- fastglm_ordinal(exposure,sparse_counts,firth=TRUE)
fastglm_po_test(sparse_fit)

An explicitly requested mean-bias-reduced null can provide finite estimates under separation. The test then evaluates the ordinary score at that null and removes the contribution associated with estimating its thresholds and common slopes. This removal is essential because the ordinary nuisance score is nonzero at a bias-reduced estimate.

#> Sample: 54 observations represented by 2 rows

This option has the same first-order chi-square limit under a regular interior null, but it can be conservative in small samples. It is an ordinary efficient-score calculation at a bias-reduced null, rather than the larger-model adjusted-score statistic discussed by Kosmidis (2014, Section 12). A higher-order accuracy claim would require a separate justification. The choice of null estimator should be specified as part of the analysis; the function never switches estimators automatically. Within fastglm_ordinal_assess(), use po.estimation="meanBR" together with po.test="score".

Bias reduction does not supply information about a predictor level that is entirely absent. Empty declared outcome categories and deficient designs therefore remain explicit unsupported cases for these tests. Failed null fits retain their convergence details. The printed explanation can be exported with as.data.frame(result,details=TRUE).

Repeated fits and large-data computation

Repeated fitting reuses calculations for several responses on a retained predictor design. Streaming instead addresses storage by rereading observations in blocks. Both approaches support common-slope and partial models with maximum likelihood or mean bias reduction, but they retain different information for later analysis.

Prepared designs and repeated outcomes

Several ordered outcomes can share the same predictors, and simulation studies often fit many responses on a fixed design. A prepared ordinal design retains the predictor matrix and its formula transformations, so these calculations can be reused across responses. Numerical computation and likelihood conventions

The numerical kernel evaluates likelihoods, scores, and information in C++. It processes probability calculations in blocks and avoids constructing a separate regression-design row for each observation and threshold. The dense input design is still retained in memory. Increasing the number of predictors or categories also increases the size of the parameter information matrix.

For logistic ordinal models, each fitting evaluation reuses calculations involving the differences between adjacent thresholds. Algebraic identities also reduce repeated exponential and logarithm calculations for category probabilities and their derivatives. These changes preserve the likelihood and bias-adjustment equations. The additional storage depends on the number of categories; it does not add an array of probabilities for every observation. The benefit depends on the predictor dimension, number of categories, and number of fitting evaluations.

The current interface supports cumulative logit, probit, and complementary log-log links, freely estimated thresholds, offsets, and frequency data. Dense models permit common slopes or selected threshold-specific slopes. An ordered factor records individual outcomes; a count matrix records the category frequencies within each predictor row. Its columns must follow the declared category order. fastglm_ordinal_streaming() fits the same common-slope or partial models by repeatedly reading blocks of observations, including the complete-data information calculation required for mean bias reduction. The large-data vignette demonstrates this interface and explains which summaries can be calculated without retaining the observations. Sparse ordinal matrices require an additional implementation.

A partial model can approach a probability boundary when the data provide little information about some threshold-specific effects. Mean bias reduction does not keep every cumulative-predictor gap bounded away from zero. In such cases, a very small remaining parameter change can coexist with an adjusted score that has not converged. The fitter checks both criteria, retains the failure diagnostics, and withholds uncertainty calculations that require convergence. Dense and streamed fitting use the same checks.

logLik() reports the ordinary categorical frequency likelihood at the returned estimate, omitting constants introduced by grouping observations. In contrast, deviance() compares the fit with a saturated model for the supplied response rows, so the grouped deviance depends on that grouping. For bias-reduced estimates, the reported likelihood is evaluated at those estimates rather than maximized by them.

Model-based Wald inference uses expected Fisher information and normal approximations. ML profile intervals and ordinary or adjusted-score tests use the first-order chi-square references described above. Robust covariance and refitting bootstrap calculations have their own regularity and category-support requirements. Nonconverged fits retain their diagnostics and point predictions, while methods requiring a covariance refuse to report uncertainty. These distinctions are useful when deciding whether a fitted distribution is sufficiently well determined for the intended interpretation.

A printed probability of zero can reflect floating-point underflow; a finite log probability distinguishes that case from a structural zero.

For a streamed fit, requesting these row details makes one additional source pass and retains at most max.rows entries in each table. Calling fastglm_diagnostics() without boundary=TRUE uses only the stored ordinal diagnostics. These tables locate potentially difficult observations; small probabilities, small gaps, and unsuccessful numerical searches do not establish that an interior solution is absent.

Missing intermediate categories within a predictor pattern

A particular boundary can be established before iterative fitting. Suppose a fully varying logit model estimates a separate set of cumulative probabilities for each distinct predictor pattern. Such a model is saturated across those patterns: the fitted probabilities at one pattern do not constrain those at another. Offsets must also be constant within each pattern for the following result.

For this model, mean bias reduction in the cumulative-logit coefficients is equivalent to adding half an observation to each endpoint category within a pattern. It adds no observations to intermediate categories, as discussed by Kosmidis (2014). Consequently, an intermediate category absent within one pattern still has zero fitted probability, even if that category is observed elsewhere. The adjacent cumulative predictors would have to coincide, so there is no solution with all category probabilities strictly positive.

The following two-pattern example has all three categories represented overall, but the middle category is absent from the first pattern:

pattern_x <- matrix(c(0,1),ncol=1,
                    dimnames=list(NULL,"group"))
pattern_counts <- rbind(c(low=6,middle=0,high=4),
                        c(low=3,middle=5,high=2))
pattern_message <- tryCatch(
    fastglm_ordinal(pattern_x,pattern_counts,nonparallel="group",firth=TRUE),
    error=conditionMessage)
pattern_message
#> [1] "this fully flexible logit model has no mean-bias-reduced solution with all category probabilities positive: an intermediate outcome category is absent within a predictor pattern: middle."

The package detects this specific configuration and explains the unavailable interior solution before starting the optimizer. Dense, prepared, and streamed fits use the same criterion; a source check reuses its initial data pass and retains only a bounded set of predictor patterns. The check uses exact pattern and offset matching and applies only to the saturated logit mean-bias-reduction case described here.

Other models can also approach a probability boundary, but a small gap or an unsuccessful numerical search does not establish that an interior solution is absent. Those fits retain their convergence diagnostics. A scientifically justified common-slope restriction changes how information is shared across predictor patterns and may give a different estimable model; it should not be selected solely to make an unsuccessful fit converge.

Category support and limits of inference

An outcome category can be absent from a sample even when it belongs to the intended scale. Removing an empty endpoint before bias reduction changes that scale. The following example retains an unobserved low category.

no_predictors <- matrix(numeric(),nrow=1,ncol=0)
endpoint_counts <- matrix(c(0,8,2),nrow=1,
                           dimnames=list(NULL,c("low","middle","high")))
endpoint_fit <- fastglm_ordinal(no_predictors,endpoint_counts,firth=TRUE)
coef(endpoint_fit,component="threshold")
#>  threshold:low|middle threshold:middle|high 
#>             -3.044522              1.223775
predict(endpoint_fit)
#>             low    middle      high
#> [1,] 0.04545455 0.7272727 0.2272727

Bias reduction retains finite thresholds in this example. Maximum likelihood would require an infinite threshold for the empty endpoint, which the current implementation does not estimate.

The same endpoint can be empty in a parametric bootstrap refit. This does not remove a threshold from the mean-bias-reduced model. The bootstrap can report uncertainty when the interior category remains represented in every refit:

endpoint_boot <- fastglm_bootstrap(
    endpoint_fit,R=199,type="parametric",seed=61105)
endpoint_boot
#> Refit bootstrap (parametric)
#> Refits: 199 of 199 successful
#> 
#> Estimates and confidence intervals:
#>                       Estimate Std. Error   lower   upper
#> threshold:low|middle   -3.0445     0.7045 -3.0445 -1.2238
#> threshold:middle|high   1.2238     0.6856  0.0000  3.0445
data.frame(
  `Category pattern`=c("Empty endpoint","Empty interior category"),
  `Proportion of refits`=c(mean(lengths(
    endpoint_boot$diagnostics$empty.end.categories)>0),

#>          Category pattern Proportion of refits
#> 1          Empty endpoint            0.6331658

For this seed, the low category is empty in some refits, but the middle category is present in every refit. The threshold parameters keep the same meaning throughout this bootstrap. If the middle category were absent, its two neighboring thresholds would coincide and the package would withhold the full-model intervals.

An empty interior category has a different consequence. We fit the reduced category model and restore the declared labels with coincident thresholds and zero probability for the removed category. This boundary interpretation follows the empty-category discussion in Kosmidis (2014, Section 8.3).

interior_counts <- matrix(c(4,0,7,3),nrow=1,
                           dimnames=list(NULL,c("low","mild","moderate","high")))
interior_fit <- fastglm_ordinal(no_predictors,interior_counts,firth=TRUE)
#> Warning: empty interior ordinal categories were collapsed for fitting; their
#> labels are retained with zero probability and coincident thresholds
coef(interior_fit,component="threshold")
#>      threshold:low|mild threshold:mild|moderate threshold:moderate|high 
#>              -0.8472979              -0.8472979               1.1895841
predict(interior_fit,se.fit=TRUE)

The warning identifies the reduction, and the fitted object records the mapping. The tied thresholds have unavailable Wald standard errors, as does the probability of the removed category. A zero fitted gap does not justify ordinary uncertainty calculations for that gap. Other probability and cumulative-probability standard errors refer to the reduced category model. The convergence checks also use its parameters, because separate score components for the tied thresholds need not vanish at this boundary.
